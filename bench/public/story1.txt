The ferry only ran twice a day, and in October it ran less than that, which is why the island felt smaller every year and why nobody who lived on it bothered to lock a door.
I learned the schedule the way you learn a scar—by pressing on it until the story came out.
My grandfather had kept the boat before me, and his grandfather before him, though nobody in the family could agree on how far back the arrangement went, and Aunt Posy had a version that involved a shipwreck and a stolen coat.
We lived on Kerrow Island, which was less an island than a rumor of one, a low green shelf of rock the sea had been arguing with for centuries and had not yet won.
Bellhaven was the only town, if you could call eleven houses, a post box, and a church a town.
The church had no bell, which everyone found funny and nobody ever explained, and on Sundays the minister rang a cowbell he kept in a drawer beside the hymn books.
In summer the tourists came for the puffins and left their paper cups along the seawall, and in winter the storms took the cups and left the puffins.
By October the visitors were gone, and the island belonged to us again, to the gulls and the wind and the slow business of waiting.
The light was automatic now, a gray steel tower that blinked whether anyone watched or not, and the old keeper’s cottage had become a shed for rope and paint.
People on the mainland liked to say the island was beautiful, and it was, in the way a knife is beautiful when you are not the one holding it.
On the morning the trouble started, I was mending a net in the shed when the telephone rang twice and stopped.
Two rings meant the mainland, which meant either a delivery or a death, and I had learned to dread both equally.
I didn’t pick it up. I had learned to recieve bad news in writing, where I could hold it at arm’s length and fold it neatly afterward.
Outside, the wind pushed the tide into the rocks with a sound like someone swallowing, and the gulls went up all at once and came down again.
Marta came down the path with a basket of eggs and the look of a woman who had already won three arguments that morning and was looking for a fourth.
“Your brother called,” she said. “He wants the boat.”
“He can want it untill the water freezes,” I said.
Marta set the basket on the bench and took off her hat, and her hair came loose in the wind like it had been waiting all morning for permission.
“He says the ferry is dying,” she said. “He says there are easier ways to lose money, and he says he knows them all.”
“There are,” I said. “But none of them come with such a view.”
She laughed, and it was the kind of laugh that made you forget for a moment that the island was shrinking and the timetable was a lie.
I remembered the summer my mother left, the way she stood at the end of the pier with a suitcase I was not allowed to carry, and how the ferry took her the way it took everyone eventually.
We had known each other since we were children, back when the crossing took 40–50 minutes and nobody on the boat carried a watch.
Now the engine was tired and half the year we ran at a loss, and the mainland had begun to talk about us the way you talk about a relative who will not move out.
I walked the length of the pier and back, past the the old crane that nobody had used since the war.
I told myself the two rings meant nothing, that the mainland called for groceries and gossip and the occasional wrong number.
But this is would explain the silence on the radio, I decided, and I let it stand.
We carried the crates into into the shed before the rain came.
Marta counted them twice and wrote the figure in a seperate ledger, which is how we stayed friends.
“You keep the books like a banker,” I said.
“And you keep the boat like a priest,” she said , and put her hat back on.
That evening I leafed trough the pages of my grandfather’s log and found nothing but weather and apologies.
Next to a boxes of spare parts sat the old compass, green with salt and still pointing somewhere true.
I peaked through the window and saw the mainland lights trembling across the water.
The radio spat static and then a voice… I turned the dial until the voice came clear.
It was my brother, calling from the city, and he sounded the way he always sounded—patient, reasonable, and wrong.
“The island is a museum,” he said. “You are the exhibit.”
“Somebody has to keep the light burning,” I said.
“Nobody needs the light,” he said. “They have radar now, and radar never sleeps and never asks for a day off.”
I definately told him he could visit, but that was in June, and June is a liar.
It was wierd to hear him talk like that, as if the water were a business decision and the house were an asset.
I couldn’t beleive he had already spoken to a broker.
The weather held until noon the next day, then turned, and I took the boat out with the mail and a crate of medicine and a list of things Marta wanted from the mainland.
The crossing was rough; the waves came over the bow in gray sheets, and I steered by memory and spite.
Halfway across I saw the mainland ferry, white and enormous, cutting toward the harbor where my slips used to be.
I killed the engine and let the old boat drift, and for a minute the whole island seemed to hold its breath.
Marta was waiting on the pier when I came back, her arms crossed, her coat dark with rain, and I learned later that she had stood there for an hour because she did not trust me to come back alone.
“They’ve scheduled a meeting,” she said. “Thursday. In the church, because it’s the only place big enough. They’re calling it a consultation, but you and I both know what it is.”
“Big enough for what?”
“For everyone who wants to tell you what to do.”
The church still had no bell, but that night it was full, and I stood at the back and listened to my neighbors argue about the future of a rock.
Somebody had brought folding chairs from the mainland, and somebody else had brought a thermos, and the two of them sat side by side like old enemies at a wedding.
My brother spoke first. He had a chart and a plan and a smile I recognized from the mainland brochures.
“We can keep the island,” he said. “We just can’t keep it the way it was.”
Marta asked him who would run the ferries, and he said ferries, plural, as if the word itself were a promise.
I thought about my grandfather, who had crossed this water in fog so thick he steered by the sound of the gulls and the feel of the swell under the keel.
I thought about the winter I was eight, when the engine failed and we drifted for six hours and nobody panicked, because nobody had ever taught us how.
When it was my turn to speak, I didn’t have a chart. I had a timetable, a compass, and a knee that ached before rain.
“The ferry runs at seven and at four,” I said. “It has run at seven and at four since before any of you were born, and it will run at seven and at four until the last of us needs it.”
Nobody clapped. Nobody argued. The thermos was passed around, and the meeting dissolved into the ordinary business of neighbors.
The meeting had not decided anything, which is what meetings are for, and everyone knew it and nobody minded.
The broker from the mainland stood by the door with a folder full of numbers, and nobody would look at him, the way you avoid a man selling something at a funeral.
My brother found me afterward and put a hand on my shoulder, and for a moment he was just my brother, and then he remembered himself and took it back.
“I’m not trying to take it from you,” he said. “I’m trying to keep it from taking you.”
“Those are the same sentence,” I said, “said in two directions.”
He smiled, and it was a sad smile, and I understood that he believed every word, which made it worse.
The wind had dropped, and the harbor lay flat as a plate, and the boats sat in it like cutlery.
I walked out onto the pier because I wanted to feel the boards under me, the old planks my father had replaced one by one, always meaning to do the rest.
A gull landed on the rail and looked at me with the flat contempt of something that had never once needed a ferry.
I told it the timetable anyway, because I had told everyone else, and I was running out of people.
It flew off, which I took as disagreement, and I went back inside.
Marta was waiting with the thermos, and she poured me a cup without asking, which is a language.
We sat on the bench where she had set the eggs that first morning, and the ordinary world went on around us, untroubled by meetings.
“He’s not wrong,” she said. “That’s the thing about him. He’s never wrong and never right at the same time.”
“I know,” I said.
“And you’re not wrong either,” she said. “Which is the problem with this island. Everyone is ‘reasonable’, and the water keeps rising.”
I laughed, and the laugh surprised me, and I let it out into the dark where it belonged.
Somewhere below us the tide turned, the way it always did, indifferent to our charts and our opinions.
We sat there until the church went dark and the last folding chair was carried back to the car, and then there was nothing left to argue about.
The harbor office had sent a surveyor the week before, a young woman with a laser and a clipboard who measured everything twice and told me I had the most well-built slips on the coast, which was the nicest thing anyone had said to me all year.
I had thanked her, and she had looked embarrassed, and we had both pretended it was about the measurements.
There were things I had never told anyone, not Marta, not my brother, not the surveyor: that I had once been offered a job on the mainland and had turned it down without opening the letter, and that I thought about it on bad nights.
The job had been in an office with a window, and the window had looked out on a parking garage, and I had decided that a parking garage was not a view, even from the fifth floor.
Maybe that was the wrong decision. Maybe the island was a slow way of losing and the mainland was a fast one.
But the ferry ran at seven and at four, and someone had to drive it, and I had never trusted anyone else with the wheel.
My grandfather used to say that a ferry is not a boat, it is a promise, and a promise is not a thing you can sell, no matter how many charts the broker brings.
I had repeated that to myself so many times that I no longer knew whether I believed it or just needed it.
That is the trouble with the things your family tells you; you inherit them before you can examine them, and by the time you can, they are load-bearing.
The island had 10–12 full-time families left, and we knew one another’s boats by the sound of their engines.
You learn a place that way, by its noises, and then one day the noises change and you cannot say exactly when it happened.
Afterward Marta walked me home along the seawall, and the rain had stopped, and the water was flat and black and kind.
“You know they’ll build the pier anyway,” she said.
“Probably,” I said. “But they’ll build it for the seven o’clock.”
She took my arm, and we walked the rest of the way without saying anything, which is how you know a friendship has earned its quiet.
In the morning the telephone rang twice and stopped, and this time I picked it up.
It was the harbor office, wanting to know the dimensions of my slip, and I told them, and then I went down to the shed and mended the net I had left undone.
