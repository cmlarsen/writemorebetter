The greenhouse sat at the end of a gravel road, and in July the gravel was so hot you could feel it through your shoes.
I had taken the job because my mother said I needed one, and because the man who owned the place, Mr. Pell, had asked for me by name.
I did not know why he had asked for me, and I did not ask, and that turned out to be the right decision.
Ashgrove was a small town in a large valley, and in summer the valley held the heat the way a bowl holds soup.
The greenhouse was hotter still, a long glass room whose enviroment changed with every hour, now damp, now dry, always green.
Mr. Pell was old and slow and certain, and he moved through the rows of plants the way a priest moves through a church.
He taught me to water from the root and not the leaf, and to talk to the seedlings in a low voice.
“They don’t understand the words,” he said. “But they understand that someone is paying attention.”
I thought that was nonsense, and then I noticed that the plants I talked to did better than the ones I didn’t.
The work was simple and endless, and I liked it more than I admitted to anyone.
There was one bed of roses that Mr. Pell tended himself, at the back, behind a curtain of vines.
He went there every evening at six and stayed until dark, and he never let me follow.
Of course I followed. I was seventeen, and a locked gate is a question, not an answer.
One evening I slipped behind the vines and watched him from the far end of the row.
He was talking to the roses, but not in the low voice he used with the seedlings. He was talking the way you talk to someone you miss.
“I know,” he said. “I know. But the summer’s almost over, and you always did hate the winter.”
I felt like a thief, and I backed away, and a watering can fell over with a sound like a bell.
He did not turn around. He said, “You can come out, Wren. I knew you were there.”
I came out, and he was not angry, which was worse.
“Who are you talking to?” I asked.
“My wife,” he said. “She planted them. She’s been gone four years, and the roses are the only thing of hers I can keep alive.”
The garden had always seemed to me a diffrent world, and now I understood it was also a memory. A world and a memory, kept in the same glass room, watered by the same hand.
I did not know what to say, so I said the truest thing I had.
“They’re beautiful,” I said. “She must have known what she was doing.”
“She did,” he said. “She knew everything about growing things. She used to say that a garden is just a conversation you have with the future.”
After that I helped him with the roses, and he let me, and we did not talk about it again.
The summer went on. The valley got hotter. The roses bloomed like they had something to prove.
In August a foriegn family moved into the house at the top of the road, and their daughter, Iman, started coming to the greenhouse.
She was my age and unimpressed by everything, which I found irresistible.
She asked questions I could not answer and did not mind when I said so.
We started walking home together, the long way, past the fields where the corn stood taller than us.
“Why do you stay here?” she asked one evening. “There’s nothing here.”
“There’s the greenhouse,” I said, which was not an answer, and she knew it.
“There’s a boy who loves a greenhouse,” she said, and I did not correct her, and I am still not sure why.
I was gratefull for her, in a way I could not have explained, because she made the town feel smaller and the world larger at the same time.
By the end of August the corn had grown to twice my hieght, or so it felt, and the roses had begun to fade.
Mr. Pell grew quiet as the days shortened, and I understood that he was afraid of the winter, not for himself but for the plants.
“They’ll come back,” I told him. “Roses always come back.”
“Not all of them,” he said. “Some are independant of the season, and some need exactly the right cold to wake.”
I said I would come back in the spring, and I meant it, and he nodded as if he had heard that promise before.
On the last day of summer, Iman and I sat on the roof of the greenhouse and watched the valley go dark.
On the table a was a cup of tea and two slices of bread, and Mr. Pell had left them there for us before he went home.
We ate and watched the valley and did not say anything about the fall, because there was nothing to say that would not sound like goodbye.
Iman laughed at something I said… and I have forgotten what it was, and I wish I hadn’t.
“My aunt says that each of the dogs are friendly,” Iman said, “but I think she has never met one.”
I did not understand the joke, and I laughed anyway, because I wanted to, and that was enough.
The winter was long and I did not visit, because the road iced over and because I was afraid of what I would find.
I worked at the diner in town instead, pouring coffee for people who did not look up, and I thought about the greenhouse every day.
I wrote Iman letters I did not send, and then I sent one, and she wrote back within the week, which surprised me.
Her letter was about the city, and about how loud it was, and about a boy she had met who reminded her of no one she knew, which she thought was the point.
I wrote back about the valley, and about the corn, and I did not mention Mr. Pell or the roses, because some things go bad when you put them on paper.
When I came back in the spring, the greenhouse was open and the roses were green and Mr. Pell was gone.
The glass was fogged and the door was unlocked, which told me he had not been gone long, or had not cared, or both.
Inside it was cold and green and alive. The seedlings had become plants, and the plants had become a jungle, and nobody had told them to stop.
The roses at the back were not dead. They were not blooming either. They were waiting, the way a person waits.
I stood there for a long time, and then I did what he would have done, which was get to work.
I watered from the root and not the leaf, and I talked to the seedlings in a low voice, and I did not know what to say, so I said the truth.
“He’s gone,” I said. “I don’t know where. I don’t know if he’s coming back.”
The roses did not answer, which I expected, and did not mind, which I did not expect.
Over the next weeks I learned the greenhouse the way you learn a language, badly at first and then all at once.
There was a notebook on the shelf, and in it were pages and pages of watering schedules and temperatures and hand-drawn diagrams of the beds.
The last page was not a schedule. It was a letter, to no one, or to anyone, in that same careful hand.
It said that he had loved the work more than he had ever said, and that he had been afraid to love it too much, because it did not last.
It said that a greenhouse is a place where you keep the future alive through the winter, and that this had been his, and now it was anyone’s who wanted it.
I read it twice, the way I read everything important, and then I put it back on the shelf where someone else could find it.
The spring came in properly that year, and the valley turned green, and the roses woke up the way they were supposed to.
Iman came home in June, and she stood at the end of the gravel road and looked at the greenhouse like it was a person she had been told stories about.
“It’s smaller than I thought,” she said.
“Everything real is,” I said.
She worked beside me that summer, and she was quicker than I was and kinder to the plants, and I learned from her the way I had learned from him.
We did not talk about him much, but we left the back bed alone, the way you leave a seat at a table.
One evening she asked me why I had stayed, and for the first time I had an answer that was not a joke.
“Because someone has to talk to them,” I said. “They don’t understand the words. But they understand that someone is paying attention.”
She looked at me for a long moment, and then she smiled, and I understood that I had become the old man in the story, which is how it is supposed to go.
The years after that went faster, the way years do once you have stopped counting them.
I got older. The town got smaller. The greenhouse, somehow, got bigger, the way a room does when you finally understand what it is for.
A note on the door said he had moved to his daughter’s house, and that the roses were mine if I wanted them.
She she had asked him to keep them, the note said, and he had kept them as long as he could.
I took the job, and I have been taking it ever since, watering from the root and talking in a low voice.
Iman left for the city, and we write sometimes, and her letters are shorter than I would like and longer than I deserve.
The town is smaller now, and the valley is the same, and the greenhouse remembers what I keep forgetting.
Every summer the roses come back, and every summer I believe them a little more.
A garden is a conversation you have with the future, and the future, it turns out, has been listening all along.
I am older now, and I know the difference between tending a thing and holding it.
You cannot hold a garden. You can only pay attention, and pay attention, and pay attention, until the season changes and you find out what you grew. That is the whole trick, and it took me twenty years and a dead man’s roses to learn it.
I hope.
