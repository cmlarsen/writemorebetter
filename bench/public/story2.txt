The shop faced the river, and in winter the river faced nothing at all, which is the kind of symmetry that only makes sense to someone who has to live with it.
I repaired clocks for a living, which is a strange way to spend a life, counting other people’s hours and never keeping any for myself.
My father had done the same work in the same room, and his tools still hung on the wall in the order he left them, an order I had never had the heart to change.
Aldermere was a town that had been important once, when the mills ran and the barges came, and now it was important only to the people who refused to leave it.
I liked the quiet. I liked the sound a mainspring makes when it finally lets go.
On the first cold day of the year, a woman brought me a clock wrapped in a towel, the way you carry something you are afraid to drop.
“It belonged to my mother,” she said. “It hasn’t run since she died.”
Her name was Ines, and she had the kind of face that made you want to tell the truth whether or not you had one.
“I can fix almost anything,” I said. “But I can’t fix what it means.”
She smiled and left the clock, and I did not see her again for a month.
In that month the river rose and the power flickered and the town held its breath, the way small towns do when the weather turns personal.
The flood came on a Tuesday, which was the day the bakery closed, so the whole street smelled of wet flour and panic.
It occured to me, standing in the doorway with a broom, that I had never actually seen the water reach the step.
The neighbors carried furniture upstairs, and somebody’s dog got loose and ran the length of the street barking at the rain.
I moved the clocks to the highest shelf and covered them with a tarp and told myself they would be fine.
They were fine. Everything was fine. That is the trouble with floods—the fear is neccessary, but the damage is optional.
By evening the water had stopped rising, and the street was a brown mirror, and Ines came back with her coat pulled tight.
“You’re still here,” she said, as if she had expected the river to take me.
“I am hard to move,” I said. “Like a grand piano. Or a grudge.”
She laughed and sat on the counter, which I did not mind, because it was the first time in years that anyone had treated the shop like a place to be rather than a place to wait.
We talked until the streetlights came on and the water began to withdraw, embarrassed by its own noise.
I told her about my father, and she told me about her mother, and between us we built a small museum of the dead and dusted it twice a week.
“You should come to dinner,” she said. “I can accomodate one more chair, and I have done it before.”
I said I would, and I meant it, which is rarer than people think.
The next week was was a blur of brass and oil and tiny screws that vanished whenever I set them down.
I worked late on the clock, because it was beautiful and broken in equal measure, the way people are.
There was a crack in the case that I filled with wax, and a bent hand that I straightened with my thumb.
The mechanism was delicate; one wrong turn and the whole thing would argue itself to death. I had seen it happen before.
This was not that. This was patience, which is just an arguement with time that you win slowly.
On the third night I found the real problem, hidden under the plate like a secret the clock had been keeping.
The clock had been dropped, long ago, and a single tooth was bent. I held it up to the light and understood that nothing in the world is as fragile as it looks.
I could not replace the tooth. The part had not been made since before my father was born.
So I filed it, slowly, on on the bench, until it fit.
The clock started. The clock stopped. The clock started again.
“You talk to it,” Ines said from the doorway, and I realized she had been watching me for some time.
“It listens better than most people,” I said.
She brought soup in a jar and set it beside the tools, and the steam climbed into the light and hung there.
“I’d like take a walk,” she said. “The water’s down, and I have been inside for days.”
We walked to the bridge and stood where the town could see us, and neither of us cared.
“People think a flooded town is a sad place,” she said. “But it’s just a town that got wet.”
I decided I liked her, which was not a decision I made lightly or quickly, and not one I was ready to say out loud.
She is seems tired, I thought, and then I saw it was not tiredness at all but grief, wearing a coat.
“My mother used to say that grief is love with nowhere to go,” she said, without my asking.
We stood there long enough for the cold to find us, and then we walked back through the wet streets past the dark windows.
The clock ran for a week, then stopped, and I opened it again and found nothing wrong at all.
Sometimes that happens. The thing simply refuses, and no amount of skill can persuade it.
I told Ines the truth, which was that I could not promise it would keep running.
“Neither can I,” she said. “And here I am, asking anyway.”
The week before the snow, I took the clock apart for the fourth time and laid the pieces on a cloth in the order they had come out.
Laying them out was a habit, a way of proving to myself that I understood the thing, that nothing had surprised me.
Ines watched from the doorway and did not speak, and I worked the way you work when someone is watching and you want them to stay.
“You make it look easy,” she said at last.
“It isn’t,” I said. “It’s just small. Small things are easier to be brave about.”
She came in and sat on the stool by the window, the one my father had used, and pulled her coat around her.
“I keep thinking about the day she died,” she said. “It wasn’t dramatic. She was in the garden, and then she wasn’t.”
I did not know what to say, so I kept working, which is the only honest thing I know how to do.
“Everyone expects you to have a story,” she said. “A last conversation. Something to hold.”
“And what do you have?” I asked.
“A clock,” she said, and laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle like a wave.
I set down my tools. There is a moment in repair when forcing it will ruin the thing, and I have learned to feel that moment coming.
“Your mother brought you here,” I said. “That’s something. Most people never bring their children anywhere.”
She looked at me for a long time, and then she nodded, and we let the silence do the rest.
The town was quiet that week in the way towns are quiet after bad news, when everyone is being careful with everyone else.
Mrs. Teodor, who runs the bakery, sent over a loaf with a note that said nothing on it at all, which was the note.
The ferryman, whose name I never learned, stood at the end of the dock every evening and smoked and watched the water go by.
I asked him once why he stood there, and he said, “Because the water is the only thing in this town that never asks me for anything.”
I thought about that for a long time. It was the truest thing anyone had said to me all winter.
Ines started coming to the shop every afternoon, and I started leaving the light on, which is a decision even when you don’t name it.
We did not talk about the clock as much as we talked around it, the way you circle a subject you are afraid to ruin.
She told me about her marriage, which had ended badly and quietly, and about a brother she had lost touch with, and about the year she spent teaching in a city where it never snowed.
I told her about my father’s hands, which had been huge and careful, and about the day he taught me to hold a screwdriver, and about the day he could no longer hold one himself.
“You loved him,” she said.
“Mostly,” I said. “The rest was complicated, which I think is what love turns into when it lasts.”
She laughed again, and this time it held, and the clock on the wall ticked through the pause like it had been waiting for us.
I do not know when I stopped thinking of the clock as her mother’s and started thinking of it as ours, but somewhere in that week it happened.
That is how grief works, I think. It moves in, and then one day it has furniture, and then one day it has a chair for you.
I have known a lot of losses, and they are not all the same size, and the people who tell you they are have not lost very much.
A clock is a small thing to hold on to, I told Ines once, and she said that was exactly why it worked. A small thing can be carried.
The bigger the thing you lose, the harder it is to keep your hands around it, and the more of it slips through your fingers.
So you hold what you can, and you are careful with it, and that is not the same as being happy, but it is close enough to get you through a winter.
The next morning there was snow,and the town looked new for about ten minutes.
Then the plows came and the illusion passed, the way all illusions do.
I finished the clock on a Sunday and set it on the counter and waited.
It ticked. It kept ticking. It ticked through the afternoon and into the dark.
When Ines came for it, she held it the way you hold a bird that has decided, against its nature, to stay.
“It’s the heal of her shoe,” she said, pointing at the tiny mark on the case. “She dropped it the day she met my father.”
I had mended the clock, but she had mended the mark’s meaning, which was more.
She paid me what I asked and then paid me more, and I let her, because refusing would have been its own kind of theft.
I did not want to embarass her by asking what the clock was worth to her, so I said nothing and watched.
“I’ll come back tomorow,” she said at the door, and she did, and the clock was still running when she came.
After she left I sat in the shop with the door open, letting the cold in the way you let an old friend in.
I thought about the hours I had spent inside other people’s clocks, and how none of them had ever been mine.
Then I did something more better than any repair: I closed the shop early and walked up the hill to see the river from above.
It was thin and silver and going somewhere, and I did not follow it. Some rivers ask you to come along; this one only asked to be watched.
I went home instead, and slept, and in the morning the clocks were all still running.
That, I have decided, is enough.
