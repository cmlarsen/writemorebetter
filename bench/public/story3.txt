The motel sat where the highway bent, and it had a sign shaped like a cactus because a cactus was the only thing the owner could draw.
My brother Theo and I ran it, which is to say we argued about it and occasionally cleaned something.
Our father built the place in the seventies, back when there was water in the ground and hope in the government forms.
Larkspur Junction was twelve miles from a town and forty from a hospital, and the sign on the road said NO SERVICES in letters a foot tall.
In summer the heat came off the asphalt in waves, and the cars that stopped were mostly lost.
In winter the wind came down off the mesa and shook the windows like it wanted in.
I had spent my whole life here, and I could tell the season by the sound the door made.
Theo wanted to sell. He had wanted to sell since before our mother died, when selling still meant something we could say out loud.
“Nobody stops anymore,” he said. “They have apps, they have maps. Everyone knows where they’re going.”
“Then we’ll be the place nobody planned on,” I said.
He didn’t answer, which is how he won arguments, by refusing to have them.
On the day the stranger came, the temperature was 108, and the ice machine had been broken since April.
I was at the desk with a fan and a crossword and a glass of tea that had gone warm an hour before.
A woman came in, middle-aged, sunburned, carrying a book and a small paper bag.
“I need a room,” she said. “I don’t care what it costs.”
I gave her room six, the one with the working air conditioner, and she paid in cash without looking at the price.
Her name was Dr. Reyna Sokolov, though she signed the register with just an R, as if the rest were optional.
She stayed three days, and in that time she barely spoke, and she never once left the room except to buy water.
I happen to run a small buisness, and I have learned that quiet guests are the best guests and also the most worrying.
On the second night I brought her a plate of food and she let me in, which surprised me more than the heat.
“You don’t have to feed me,” she said. “I’m not a stray.”
“Everyone’s a stray out here,” I said. “It’s the only qualification.”
She laughed, a short, dry sound, and gestured at the bed, where her book lay open like a flag.
She had been my father’s freind, it turned out, long before I was born.
“We met at a conference,” she said. “Your father gave a paper on groundwater, and then the goverment gave him a grant, and then the grant disappeared.”
I did not know my father had ever held a pencil, let alone given a paper.
“He was a hydrologist,” she said. “Before the motel. Before everything.”
That is how it happend, the way the past always happens, sideways and without warning.
She told me my father had predicted the wells would fail, and that nobody had listened, and that he had bought this land to prove them wrong.
“And did he?” I asked.
“He proved them wrong,” she said. “And then he proved them right. That’s what a lifetime is.”
I felt something move in my chest, some stubborn thing, and I immediatly went to the kitchen to keep my hands busy.
I know I should have asked her more that night, but I let her sleep, and the chance went with the morning.
The next day was hotter still, and the wind came from the south and pushed the dust along the road in long, slow flags.
Theo read the register and saw the name and went pale, and I understood that he knew things he had not told me.
“She was at the funeral,” he said. “You were too young. You wouldn’t remember.”
“I remember the whole thing,” I said, which was a lie, and he knew it, and he let it stand anyway.
On the third night the wind rose , then the screen door slammed, and Reyna came to the desk with the book in her hands.
“This was his,” she said. “He left it with me the last time he drove north. I meant to give it back.”
The book was a field journal, bound in green cloth, and inside were years and years of the same argument in the same careful impossible hand.
“The we drove north,” the last entry began, “and the wells were dry, and I understood that I had been right, which is the worst thing to be.”
I could of cried, but I didn’t. I made coffee instead, and we sat in the office until the sky turned the color of a bruise.
She told me he had loved this place, and that he had loved our mother, and that he had been afraid of both.
“Your welcome here anytime,” I said, and meant it, though the grammar of it embarrassed me even then.
He book was heavy in my hands, and I held it the way you hold something you are not allowed to keep.
To my knowlege he never came back after that last trip, though she said he did, once, in a year I was away.
I wanted to to tell Theo that night, but I didn’t know how, so I told him nothing at all.
After Reyna went to bed that third night, I sat in the office and read the journal by the light of the desk lamp, page by page.
My father’s handwriting got worse as the years went on, as if the land itself were pressing down on his letters.
He had drawn maps of the wells, and marked them with dates and depths and small, precise disappointments.
There was a page for the year I was born, and beside it, in the margin, just one word: enough.
I did not know what it meant, and I did not want to guess, and I read it three times anyway.
Theo found me there at two in the morning, and for once he did not say anything about the books or the bills.
He pulled up a chair and read over my shoulder, and we went through the journal together, the way we had never done anything before.
“He knew,” Theo said. “He knew the whole time.”
“He knew,” I said. “And he stayed. That’s the part I can’t get past.”
We talked about our father in a way we never had, carefully at first and then not carefully at all.
Theo said he remembered the smell of the man’s coat, and I said I remembered his whistle, and neither of us could remember his voice.
It is strange how the dead go in pieces, a scent here, a sound there, and never the whole shape of them.
We were still talking when the sky turned gray, and the desert began its long slow impersonation of the sea.
By sunrise we had a plan, which is a dangerous thing for two people who have spent their lives avoiding one.
The plan was this: we would keep the motel, fix what could be fixed, and stop apologizing for the parts of it that were falling down.
“And the cactus sign?” Theo asked.
“The cactus sign stays,” I said. “It’s the only thing on this property with personality.”
He laughed, and it was an old laugh, one I had not heard since we were children and the world was small enough to fit in the back of a truck.
I did not tell Theo everything she had said, because some things were mine now, and I was learning how to own them.
The months after that were not easy, and some of them were bad, and one of them was the worst I have had.
The pump failed in July, and the well ran dry in August, and by September we were hauling water from town in the back of Theo’s truck.
“This is the part where we sell,” he said, and then he went out and fixed the pump anyway.
I learned that a man can mean one thing and do another and still be telling the truth.
We got by on guests who were lost, and on the ones who were looking for something and did not know its name.
A trucker from Abilene stayed a week and told us about a daughter he had not spoken to in years, and left a phone number with no area code.
A couple on their honeymoon ran out of gas and stayed two nights and left us a bottle of wine we never opened.
The desert does not care who passes through it, and I began to find that comforting rather than cruel.
That winter I read the journal so many times that I could have recited the maps from memory, and maybe I did, aloud, walking the empty rooms.
Theo heard me once and did not laugh. He just stood in the doorway with his coffee, listening, like a man attending a service.
We were learning, both of us, how to be the children of someone who had been right, which is a strange inheritance.
There is no money in being right. There is a kind of quiet, and the quiet has to be enough.
In the morning she was gone, and the key was on the desk, and there was a note that said simply thank you.
Theo came in at noon and found me reading, and for once he did not say anything about selling.
He sat down across from me and poured himself a cup of the coffee I had made too strong.
“What does it say?” he asked.
“It says the ground remembers,” I said. “It says we should have listened to the old man.”
We sat there a long time. The fan turned. The sun crossed the floor and climbed the far wall and let itself out through the curtain.
After a while Theo said, “We could fix the ice machine.”
“We could,” I said. “It would cost a fortune.”
“We’ve got a fortune,” he said. “It’s just all in rooms nobody rents.”
I laughed for the first time in a week, and then I got up and put the journal in the safe behind the desk, where my father used to keep nothing at all. The safe had been empty for 12–15 years, and the journal made it the richest thing in the county.
The town never grew back, and the water never came back, and every summer the sign still says NO SERVICES, which is true and also a joke we never tire of.
But we painted the cactus that spring…and we fixed the ice machine, and we started leaving the porch light on for people who had not planned to stop.
Sometimes they stop, and sometimes they don’t, and either way the light is on. That is the whole of our business now, and it is enough.
A year later a package came, no return address, and inside was a photograph of my father standing in front of this very motel, squinting, happy, and impossibly young.
On the back, in that same careful hand, were three words: water is patient.
I hung it behind the desk, and now and then Theo and and I stand there and look at it, and I tell him the truth, which changes a little every time.
The motel is still here. So are we. The desert is still removing everything else, one grain at a time, with enormous patience.
That is the only kind of permanence I believe in anymore, which is maybe the only kind that was ever real.
